# Storyboard Interpretation

## Evidence hierarchy

Use evidence in this order:

1. the moving storyboard frames and their timing;
2. preceding and following Panels, then the complete sequence;
3. burned-in dialogue, labels, sound cues, and explicit screen notes;
4. the script and other references for narrative context.

The script can establish who, where, why, or what an object is. It does not override an action, expression, rhythm, staging choice, or spatial presentation that the storyboard visibly redesigned.

## Two-pass reading

### Visual-first pass

Before using script wording, describe the sequence in neutral visual terms:

- who initiates each action and what physically happens next;
- whether a subject actually moves or is only revealed by camera movement, foreground reframing, reflection, or a display image;
- where the action occurs and whether the view shifts between interior, exterior, threshold, overhead, POV, projection, memory, or imagined space;
- the exact setup, anticipation, turn, payoff, and recovery beats;
- posture, acceleration, hesitation, overshoot, recoil, facial change, and other performance information;
- what creates tension, surprise, comedy, elegance, awkwardness, or contrast;
- which facts are visible, which are inferred from continuity, and which remain uncertain.

Use motion and timing, not a single representative frame, to understand temporal actions.

### Script reconciliation

After the visual pass, read the relevant full script and sequence:

- confirm character identity, object identity, narrative objective, dialogue context, and offscreen continuity;
- compare the script's intended beat with the storyboard's chosen visual solution;
- keep the storyboard solution when it compresses, replaces, reverses, or improves the scripted action;
- never restore a script action that is absent from the picture.

If the evidence is genuinely ambiguous, mark it `待确认` or ask the user. Do not hide ambiguity behind confident script-derived wording.

## Read performance as designed action

Performance annotations should capture authored design, especially when the meaning depends on multiple beats. Useful patterns include:

- imposing preparation followed by a trivial solution, creating expectation and comic reversal;
- a confident charge followed by a dodge, overshoot, loss of balance, and rescue;
- a calm controlled action contrasted with another character's panic;
- an apparently dangerous move resolved by a clean, elegant escape.

Segment the note at meaningful turns. Do not stretch one label across later beats that change action, performance, or viewpoint. Describe the observable rhythm and contrast without inventing private motivation.

## Read space shot by shot

Do not mechanically inherit the previous shot's `空间类型`. Re-evaluate the represented space whenever framing crosses a threshold or changes viewpoint.

- Distinguish a character's physical location from the space shown to the viewer.
- Separate an interior window edge from an exterior downward view, even when the action is continuous.
- Treat a projection, device view, POV, reflection, or imagined insert as its displayed space when that distinction matters to comprehension.
- Attribute a reveal to the camera when the subject remains in place. Do not rewrite a pull-back, pan, or reframing as the subject entering the frame.
- Update scene information only where the spatial change helps a reader understand the action; avoid redundant repetition.

## Semantic self-check

Before authoring overlays or Base records, ask:

- Could this sentence have been written without watching the storyboard? If yes, rewatch and make it picture-specific.
- Does the annotation preserve a visual invention that differs from the script?
- Does its time range include a later reversal, new action, or new space that needs a separate event?
- Is every asserted action actually visible or securely established by adjacent Panels?
- Does the placement leave the performance beat, face, original dialogue, timecode, and Scene/Panel labels readable?
